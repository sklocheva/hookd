Mill yarns, also called deadstock or cone yarns, come from manufacturers who produce yarn mainly for large-scale or high-end producers. Factories usually produce more than they need to account for errors in the production process and provide the best quality. Some of that yarn is left unused and, rather than let the fine fibers go to waste, they sell it to a reseller who makes it available to a wider audience. Smaller and local mills may also sell directly to end customers, so do keep an eye on what is locally available, too!

Resold mill yarns are often a finite resource. If you see something you like, act fast and buy a little more than you think you'll need, because if you don't, there's a real chance you'll never see that yarn again. I never encourage an impulse purchase, but still go in with a cool head and know that if you don't plan accordingly, you are setting yourself up for an unfortunate game of yarn chicken, and not one you want to play.

Another thing to keep in mind is that mill yarns are often produced for machine knitting, so they tend to be much finer. Read the label carefully when buying online, especially if you have a specific project in mind. Even after reading the label and trying to decipher it, I still ended up with a chainette (a tube-like construction) that didn't work for my pattern. I loved the yarn anyway and used it right away. I just had to pivot the project a bit.

But that unpredictability is also one of my favorite things about mill yarn: finding things you'd rarely see in a regular shop. Resellers who carry mill yarns tend to have a wide variety of fiber types like cotton, merino, alpaca, cashmere, angora. What I love most is the range of designer yarns that experiment with texture and feel and that's exactly the kind of thing a conventional shop rarely carries. My all-time favorite find so far: a cheap, fine alpaca yarn with actual glass beads worked right in. But I have also purchased interesting fibers like geelong wool, mako cotton, cashmere and silk, and I got to try them without breaking the bank.

You still have to keep in mind that it takes a bit more effort and not only because they are a finite source. They can behave differently from what you're used to. Some industrial yarns are treated with oils or other finishes to help them run smoothly through textile machinery. Once those finishes wash out, the yarn can change quite a bit and become softer, fluffier, or drape differently than it did on the cone. Not to mention that the cones are usually very tightly wound. So never judge a book by it's cone! This is exactly why swatching and washing/blocking the swatch is an absolute must with these yarns.

Sometimes you find a treasure - an incredible luxury yarn for next to nothing and you make something gorgeous with it. Sometimes you find the perfect cone and it turns out to be completely unsuitable for what you had in mind. Either way, you end up with something fun to play with and for me, that's always worth it.

## Quick tips for buying mill yarn

- **Buy more than you think you need.** Mill yarns are often sold in one-off, finite lots. Once it's gone, it's gone.
- **Read the label closely**, especially online. Many mill yarns are spun for machine knitting and run finer than expected.
- **Swatch and wash before committing!** Industrial finishes can wash out and change how the yarn behaves drastically.
- **Keep an open mind on the project.** The yarn you buy may not suit the pattern you had in mind.
